## Abstract

Field monitoring stations often operate under a limited power budget that prevents all measurement channels from running simultaneously, making it necessary to decide which channels to activate at each time step. Existing approaches to this problem typically aim to reduce state estimation error, minimise the staleness of measurements, or follow a fixed sampling pattern. This paper addresses a different objective: selecting channels so as to minimise the error of a downstream time-series forecasting model. To this end, a reinforcement learning-based scheduler called prediction-driven proximal policy optimisation (PD-PPO) is proposed. At each scheduling step, PD-PPO scores every channel using a summary of the current state estimates, their associated uncertainties, how long ago each channel was last observed, the previous channel selection, and contextual indicators of ongoing physical events. These scores are then mapped to a feasible set of active channels that satisfies the power budget and a set of operating rules. The training reward is the forecast error produced by a pre-trained, fixed forecasting model evaluated on the partial observations collected under the chosen channel set. To prevent data leakage, the forecasting model, the scheduling policy, the static reference designs, and the final test are each fitted or evaluated on separate, non-overlapping time periods. The method is evaluated on a physically grounded simulation benchmark representing an Antarctic automatic weather station (AWS) used for blowing-snow monitoring. In this benchmark, PD-PPO activates all eight available channels at intermediate rates without locking any channel permanently on or off. Across ten independent test runs, it outperforms the best rule-based dynamic baseline in all ten runs and the best competing scheduler subject to the same duty-cycle constraint in nine out of ten runs. Fixed channel subsets remain competitive and serve as important reference designs. The results demonstrate that prediction-driven reinforcement learning can learn forecast-preserving channel selection strategies when the power budget and operating rules are enforced at the point of action selection.

**Keywords:** Sensor scheduling; Power-constrained scheduling; Time-series forecasting; Deep reinforcement learning; Duty cycle; Blowing-snow monitoring

## 1. Introduction

Field monitoring stations often combine low-cost background sensors with higher-cost specialised instruments. A typical station might include weather, radiation, surface-temperature, particle, and snow-flux channels, yet the available power may allow only a subset of those channels to operate during any given measurement interval. The general problem is sensor scheduling under a power budget: a controller must decide which channels to activate at each scheduling step.

Different objectives are possible for this decision. A controller can minimise the covariance of a state estimator, reduce the age of information (AoI) — a measure of how stale the most recent observation of each variable is — follow a fixed sampling pattern, or optimise a downstream task. This paper focuses on the forecasting objective, where the quality of a channel selection is judged by its effect on the accuracy of a subsequent time-series prediction.

This distinction matters for stations with heterogeneous channels. Low-cost channels may provide useful background context at most times, while specialised channels may be slow to start up or most informative only during specific physical events such as blowing-snow episodes. Because of this, a fixed channel subset can be a strong reference design: it requires no online learning and can still capture the most informative channels for the dominant operating conditions. A learned scheduler must therefore be compared not only against cycling or random policies, but also against well-chosen fixed designs.

The forecasting version of sensor scheduling is formulated here as a sequential decision problem. At each scheduling step, the controller selects a binary subset of the available channels. The selected subset must satisfy the power budget and a set of operating rules, including a minimum number of consecutive steps for which a channel must remain active once switched on, per-channel duty-cycle limits, and a bound on the switching rate. Schedule quality is measured by the forecast error over future time steps rather than by the instantaneous estimation error alone.

The proposed method, prediction-driven proximal policy optimisation (PD-PPO), is a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) scheduler for this setting. The policy receives a summary of the current state estimates, their uncertainties, the time elapsed since each channel was last observed (i.e., the per-channel AoI), the previous channel selection, and event context. It outputs one score per channel, and a projection step converts those scores into a feasible binary channel selection that satisfies the power budget and operating rules. During training, a fixed forecasting model — pre-trained on an earlier portion of the data and held frozen throughout policy learning — evaluates the partial observations collected under the chosen channels and returns a forecast error as the training reward. Because the forecasting model is fixed, every candidate policy is judged by the same scoring function.

The evaluation benchmark instantiates this scheduling problem in the context of Antarctic blowing-snow monitoring. The simulated station contains heterogeneous microclimate and snow-transport channels with different power costs and activation delays. This benchmark is well-suited for the study because the forecast-relevant variables operate on different time scales: background weather channels are broadly useful, while particle and snow-flux channels become most informative around blowing-snow events. The setting therefore tests whether a learned scheduler can preserve forecast quality without collapsing to a fixed subset or resorting to unrealistically rapid switching.

The results show that the power budget and operating rules have a substantial effect on which scheduling strategy performs best. Under a purely instantaneous power budget, fixed subsets selected on a validation period are strong reference designs. When minimum activation time and duty-cycle rules are added, PD-PPO learns low-switching schedules that keep all eight channels in use at intermediate rates. Across ten independent test runs, it outperforms the best rule-based dynamic baseline in every run and the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in nine runs. The fixed subset baseline remains a necessary reference, while the learned scheduler provides a consistent advantage over cycling and AoI-based dynamic policies.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, a forecasting-oriented formulation of sensor scheduling under a power budget is introduced, in which the training reward is the future forecast error under partial observation. Second, a projected PPO scheduler is proposed that enforces the power budget and operating rules at the point of action selection rather than through soft penalties. Third, a chronological evaluation protocol is described that keeps the forecasting model fitting, policy training, static reference selection, and final testing on strictly separate time periods. Fourth, a controlled blowing-snow benchmark is provided that shows when adaptive scheduling improves over rule-based dynamic baselines and when fixed subset designs remain strong references.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1. Resource-constrained sensing

Sensor scheduling under resource constraints has been studied extensively, with common objectives including the reduction of state estimation covariance, maximisation of information gain, and minimisation of the age of information (AoI). AoI quantifies how stale the most recent observation of a variable is: it increases by one at each time step and resets to zero when the channel is observed. Representative work in this area includes scheduling policies based on Markov decision processes for energy-harvesting sensors (Fernández-Bes et al., 2015), Q-learning approaches for multi-target tracking (Qu et al., 2022), deep reinforcement learning methods for monitoring dynamical systems (Alali et al., 2024), Kalman-filter-based optimal selection with auxiliary dynamics (Al Ahdab et al., 2025), Whittle-index reinforcement learning for wireless sensor networks (Jonah et al., 2026), and learning-based scheduling for delay-aware remote state estimation (Tran et al., 2026). These objectives are appropriate when the goal is to maintain a current state estimate or to refresh stale measurements.

In the forecasting formulation used in this paper, a channel can be valuable because it improves a later prediction, even if it is not the oldest or most uncertain channel at the current step. This distinction is important for stations with heterogeneous channels. Background channels may be cheap and broadly useful. Event-sensitive channels may be costly, slow to start, or most informative only around a specific physical event. A fixed subset can therefore be a strong reference design, and a learned scheduler should be tested against that design as well as against cycling or random policies.

### 2.2. Learning-based scheduling policies

Deep reinforcement learning has been widely applied to scheduling problems in which the system state is dynamic and the action space is combinatorial. Within the Expert Systems with Applications literature, scheduling problems are commonly formulated in terms of a system state, a scheduling action, a reward signal, and a set of baseline dispatching rules — for example in flexible job-shop scheduling, production scheduling, and satellite task scheduling. The present paper follows the same structure: the action is a feasible subset of the available channels, and the reward is the effect of that subset on downstream forecast accuracy.

Reinforcement learning is attractive for this problem because the forecast error is delayed and state-dependent: the value of activating a channel now depends on what the forecasting model will need several steps later. However, soft penalty terms alone are insufficient when schedules must satisfy a hard power budget at execution time. A learned policy must produce feasible schedules at test time, not merely receive a lower training reward for infeasible actions. PD-PPO therefore uses an action projection step that maps the policy's channel scores to a feasible selection before execution, and schedule behaviour — including per-channel duty cycles and the switching rate — is reported alongside forecast error (Schulman et al., 2017; Murad et al., 2020; Wei and Zheng, 2020; Ogbodo et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2024; Pendyala et al., 2024; Ibrahim et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2022).

### 2.3. Blowing-snow monitoring as a benchmark

The application benchmark is a simulated Antarctic automatic weather station (AWS) for blowing-snow monitoring. Antarctic AWS networks provide long-term atmospheric and surface observations, but spatial and seasonal coverage remains uneven (Wille et al., 2023). Blowing-snow studies require variables whose forecast value changes with storm conditions, near-surface transport, and particle microstructure (Amory, 2020; Sharma et al., 2023; Monrad-Krohn et al., 2026).

The benchmark maps this domain onto eight logical channels with heterogeneous power costs and activation delays. It is a controlled scheduling testbed in which the same underlying truth sequence can be replayed under different channel selection strategies, so that differences in forecast error are attributable to the schedule rather than to meteorological sampling variability.

### 2.4. Forecasting models as schedule evaluators

Time-series forecasting models provide a direct way to score channel selections by their prediction consequences (Lim et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024). In PD-PPO, a fixed forecasting model is trained on an early chronological portion of the data and then held frozen. During policy training, this model returns the forecast error over several future time steps for the partial observations available under the current channel selection. Because the forecasting model is not updated during policy training, all candidate policies are compared through the same scoring function, and strict temporal separation among model fitting, policy training, validation, and testing is required.

## 3. Problem Formulation

### 3.1. Sensor scheduling under a power budget

Let $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the simulated system state at scheduling step $t$. The state contains meteorological and blowing-snow variables, including wind speed, wind direction, air temperature, relative humidity, atmospheric pressure, solar radiation, snow-surface temperature, particle statistics, and snow mass flux. A channel selection is a binary vector $a_t \in \{0,1\}^m$ over $m$ logical channels. A logical channel is a data stream that can be scheduled independently; one physical instrument may expose more than one logical channel if its streams have different power costs or forecast value.

Each channel $i$ has a normalised steady-state power cost $p_i$, a startup power cost $p_i^{\text{peak}}$, and an activation delay $\tau_i$. After the activation delay has elapsed, the active channel contributes observations of its associated variables. When a channel is inactive, the state estimator propagates the variable from the most recent valid observation. The forecasting model receives the estimated state, the observation mask indicating which channels are active, and derived features. The objective is to minimise future forecast error under partial sensing.

For clarity, the following terminology is used consistently throughout the paper. A *static subset* keeps one validation-selected fixed channel combination active at all times. A *static-priority* schedule replays that same fixed combination subject to the same duty-cycle limits as the learned scheduler. The *duty cycle* of a channel is the fraction of scheduling steps during which it is active. The *minimum activation time* is the minimum number of consecutive steps for which a channel must remain active once it has been switched on. The *fixed forecasting model* is the pre-trained model used to score channel selections during both training and evaluation.

### 3.2. Power budget and operating rules

The basic power constraint requires that the total steady-state cost of the active channels at each step does not exceed a budget $B$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i a_{t,i} \leq B. \tag{1}$$

Newly activated channels must also satisfy a startup feasibility check, since starting up a channel draws a higher instantaneous current:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i^{\text{peak}} a_{t,i} \mathbf{1}\{a_{t-1,i} = 0\} \leq B^{\text{peak}}. \tag{2}$$

The feasible action set is denoted $\mathcal{A}_B(t, a_{t-1})$ because both the startup check and the operating rules depend on the previous channel selection.

The experiments with operating rules add three further requirements. First, once a channel is switched on, it must remain active for at least the configured minimum activation time. Second, each channel must stay within a prescribed duty-cycle range over the evaluation window. Third, the switching rate — the fraction of steps at which the active channel set changes — is bounded to prevent rapid cycling. All three rules are enforced when the channel selection is made, not merely penalised after execution.

### 3.3. Energy-account extension

Some remote stations can accumulate energy before a storm and draw on that reserve during event windows. To test this mechanism separately, a simplified energy account is modelled with state of charge (SOC) $s_t$, a constant energy harvest rate $h$, a battery capacity $C$, and a minimum reserve $s_{\min}$. The power drawn at step $t$ is

$$c_t(a_t) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i a_{t,i}, \tag{3}$$

and the account evolves as

$$s_{t+1} = \text{clip}_{[0,C]}\!\left(s_t + h - c_t(a_t)\right). \tag{4}$$

The startup feasibility check in Equation (2) still applies. Channel selections that would reduce the account below $s_{\min}$ are rejected before execution. This model is a scheduling abstraction rather than a detailed battery-physics model.

### 3.4. Forecast error as the scheduling objective

The fixed forecasting model maps an input window produced by a channel selection to predictions of selected target variables over $H$ future steps. Let $\hat{y}_{t+k,j}$ denote the prediction for variable $j$ at future step $k$, and $y_{t+k,j}$ the corresponding ground truth. Let $w_k \geq 0$ be forecast-horizon weights, $\alpha_j \geq 0$ target weights, and $\sigma_j > 0$ target scales. The primary evaluation metric is the forecast-weighted mean absolute error (FW-MAE):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FW}}(t) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{H} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{Y}} w_k \alpha_j \left|\hat{y}_{t+k,j} - y_{t+k,j}\right| / \sigma_j}{\sum_{k=1}^{H} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{Y}} w_k \alpha_j}. \tag{5}$$

Lower FW-MAE indicates better forecasting under the executed channel selection. The policy reward combines the forecast error with penalties for switching and operating-rule violations:

$$r_t = -\left[\omega_t \mathcal{L}_{\text{FW}}(t) + \lambda_{\text{sw}} S_t + \lambda_{\text{duty}} D_t + \lambda_{\text{viol}} V_t\right], \tag{6}$$

where $S_t$ is the switching cost, $D_t$ is the duty-cycle violation, and $V_t$ collects feasibility or energy-budget violations. The optional event weight $\omega_t$ equals $\eta_{\text{event}}$ during detected blowing-snow events and 1 otherwise. The main reported experiment with operating rules uses $\eta_{\text{event}} = 1$.

### 3.5. Forecast error versus age of information as scheduling objectives

AoI and estimation covariance are useful scheduling objectives, but they optimise different quantities from forecast error. Let $\pi$ be a feasible scheduling policy. Its forecast-loss value is

$$J_{\text{fcst}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t \mathcal{L}_{\text{FW}}(t)\right]. \tag{7}$$

AoI-based and covariance-based policies instead optimise

$$J_{\text{AoI}}(\pi) = -\mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{Y}} \text{age}_{t,j}\right], \qquad J_{\text{cov}}(\pi) = -\mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_t \text{tr}(P_t)\right]. \tag{8}$$

These objectives can rank the same channel selections differently when a delayed channel is most valuable just before an event window.

**Proposition 1** (Non-equivalence of forecast-loss reward). *Forecast-loss reward, estimator covariance trace, and per-variable AoI can induce different orderings over feasible channel-selection schedules. In particular, there exist two feasible policies $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ such that $\pi_1$ has lower AoI or lower covariance trace, while $\pi_2$ has strictly lower forecast error.*

*Proof sketch.* Consider a two-variable process with a slowly varying background variable $z_t$ and an event variable $e_t$. A low-cost channel observes $z_t$ frequently, while a delayed event-sensitive channel observes $e_t$. If the forecast target is $y_{t+1} = c z_t + M e_t + \eta_t$ with $M \gg c$, a schedule that refreshes only $z_t$ can have lower AoI or covariance trace while missing the intervals where $e_t = 1$. A second schedule can accept older background measurements but activate the event-sensitive channel before the event, yielding lower forecast error whenever the event term dominates the background error. $\square$

## 4. Prediction-Driven Scheduling Method

### 4.1. State representation, action selection, and feasibility projection

PD-PPO is a scheduling policy for the problem defined in Section 3. At each scheduling step, the policy receives a state summary containing the current state estimates $\hat{x}_t$, the diagonal of the estimation error covariance matrix $\text{diag}(P_t)$, the per-channel AoI, the previous channel selection, the current energy account state (when applicable), and event context. The policy outputs one score per channel. A projection step then converts those scores into a feasible binary channel selection that satisfies the power budget and all operating rules.

This projection step is central to the method. The learned policy is never rewarded for a channel selection that could not be executed by the sensing system. The same rules are applied when evaluating all baseline policies, so forecast error and schedule feasibility are compared under a common rule set.

The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 1. The runtime scheduling loop maps the state summary to a feasible channel selection via the PD-PPO policy and the projection step, executes that selection in the AWS simulator, and updates the state estimator with the resulting observations. The training loop uses the fixed forecasting model to score the partial-observation trajectory collected over a rollout window, computes the FW-MAE-based reward, and updates the policy parameters via PPO.

### 4.2. Forecast-based reward

The reward follows Equation (6). The dominant term is the forecast error returned by the fixed forecasting model. Switching penalties, duty-cycle penalties, and feasibility penalties shape the policy toward channel selections that are both useful for forecasting and executable under the stated rules. State estimation error is not the training objective in the main experiments; it is retained only as a supporting diagnostic.

The forecasting model is trained before policy learning and then held frozen. It therefore acts as a common scoring function: all candidate channel selections are compared through the same model on the same held-out ground-truth windows.

### 4.3. Policy update

Let $o_t$ denote the scheduler observation, $a_t$ the projected channel selection executed at step $t$, and $\hat{A}_t$ the advantage estimate computed by generalised advantage estimation (GAE). PPO updates the policy with the clipped surrogate objective

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\!\left(\rho_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right], \tag{9}$$

where the probability ratio is

$$\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid o_t)}. \tag{10}$$

The full training loss adds a value function loss, an entropy regularisation term, and two auxiliary training guides:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PD-PPO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}} + c_v \mathcal{L}_V - c_H \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta) + \beta_{\text{BC}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{AWBC}} + \beta_{\text{KL}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{guide}}. \tag{11}$$

The first auxiliary guide is an advantage-weighted behavioural cloning (AWBC) loss. For labelled training states, a greedy reference action $g_t$ is obtained by evaluating a small set of feasible candidate channel selections with the fixed forecasting model and selecting the one with the lowest forecast error:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{AWBC}} = -\frac{\sum_t \ell_t [\hat{A}_t]_+ \log \pi_\theta(g_t \mid o_t)}{\sum_t \ell_t [\hat{A}_t]_+ + \varepsilon}, \tag{12}$$

where $\ell_t \in \{0,1\}$ marks labelled states and $[\cdot]_+$ denotes the positive part. This term encourages the policy to imitate high-value greedy actions, weighted by how much better those actions are than the current policy's average.

The second auxiliary guide keeps the learned distribution close to a candidate-policy distribution $q(a \mid o_t)$ that is fitted from candidate rollouts on the policy-training partition:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{guide}} = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\text{KL}\!\left(q(\cdot \mid o_t) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_t)\right)\right]. \tag{13}$$

Together, these two guides improve exploration over the combinatorial space of feasible channel subsets. Neither guide uses data from the final test partition. An SOC prediction auxiliary head is available for energy-account experiments but is disabled in the main comparison with operating rules.

### 4.4. Chronological evaluation protocol

Each generated ground-truth sequence is divided into four non-overlapping chronological partitions:

1. forecasting model fitting on the first 35% of the sequence;
2. policy training and candidate-guide fitting on the next 50%;
3. validation-based static subset selection on the next 7.5%;
4. final testing on the last 7.5%, using non-overlapping 1024-step windows.

The final test windows are held out from all preceding stages. This strict temporal separation ensures that the forecasting model, the scheduling policy, and the static reference designs are each fitted on data that precedes the test period, preventing any form of look-ahead bias.

### 4.5. Baseline policies

The fixed-budget comparison uses five reference policies. Full observation is an unconstrained reference that is not feasible under the reported power budget. The static subset selects the best feasible fixed channel combination on the validation windows. Round-robin cycles through feasible channel subsets in a fixed order. The AoI policy prioritises channels with the largest AoI at each step. Random scheduling samples a feasible channel selection uniformly at random.

The comparison with operating rules adds rule-matched baselines. The static-priority baseline replays the validation-selected static subset subject to the same duty-cycle limits as PD-PPO. Rule-based dynamic baselines are evaluated both in their original unconstrained form and under the same operating rules as PD-PPO. This design separates three distinct questions: whether a learned policy outperforms unconstrained cycling policies; whether it outperforms dynamic policies under the same operating rules; and how close it comes to a strong fixed subset design.

## 5. Simulation and Evaluation Setup

### 5.1. Channels of the sensing system

The benchmark is a controlled cold-region sensing system simulation based on an Antarctic automatic weather station (AWS) use case, though the scheduling formulation is general. Five instrument families are mapped to eight logical channels so that streams with different power costs or forecast value can be scheduled independently. Table 1 lists the channels and their normalised scheduling costs.

Figure 3 shows the rendered station platform that motivated the channel design. The five physical instrument families are represented as eight logical channels because multi-output instruments expose separable data streams with different sampling behaviour or activation delays.

### 5.2. Ground-truth sequence generation

Each random seed generates a multivariate ground-truth sequence containing wind speed, wind direction, air temperature, relative humidity, atmospheric pressure, solar radiation, snow-surface temperature, particle statistics, and snow mass flux. The generator is calibrated against Antarctic AWS observations and published blowing-snow statistics (Wille et al., 2023; Amory, 2020; Sharma et al., 2023; Monrad-Krohn et al., 2026). Blowing-snow events are produced by a semi-Markov process coupled to wind exceedance, so event labels mark periods during which the particle and snow-flux channels become more valuable for forecasting.

The observation model maps the ground-truth state to the logical channels in Table 1. Each channel has observation noise, an activation delay, and normalised steady-state and startup power costs. The costs are scheduling costs representing relative operating burden in the simulator, not measured watt-level loads. The fixed-budget experiment varies $B \in \{1.65, 1.70, 1.75\}$. The main experiment with operating rules uses $B = 1.70$ with common minimum activation time and duty-cycle limits. The energy-account experiment allows stored energy to be spent during event windows, changing the feasible set over time.

Generator validation checks cover meteorological structure and blowing-snow behaviour: wind autocorrelation, event frequency, scalar-distribution agreement with station reference data, event durations, flux–wind scaling, particle correlation, and wind-speed spectra (Aloni et al., 2024). These checks define the benchmark boundary; they are not used to select final-test policies. Table 2 and Figure 4 summarise the checks.

### 5.3. Forecasting targets and evaluation metric

The forecasting task covers microclimate targets and snow-transport variables. The primary metric is FW-MAE over target variables and forecast horizons. Physical-unit errors, event-period loss, warm-up aborts, power consumption, per-channel duty cycles, and switching rates are also reported. Final-test FW-MAE is the ranking metric.

The fixed forecasting model is a temporal convolutional network (TCN) with instance normalisation, trained only on the forecasting-model fitting partition. Across the ten main seeds for the operating-rule comparison, its final smooth-L1 training loss is $0.07455 \pm 0.00253$ and its validation loss is $0.07315 \pm 0.00285$ on held-out windows from the same partition. This gap is used as a sanity check before the scheduler comparison; policy ranking uses the separate final-test partition.

### 5.4. Statistical comparisons

The fixed-budget experiment evaluates each budget level over ten seeds. Pairwise comparisons use paired nonparametric tests with Bonferroni correction within the reported comparison family. The experiment with operating rules reports paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for the two dynamic-baseline families.

## 6. Results

### 6.1. Scheduling under operating rules

Table 3 and Figure 5 report the main comparison with operating rules. All policies in this experiment face the same power budget, minimum activation time, duty-cycle range, and feasibility projection. The comparison therefore measures both forecast quality and whether the resulting schedule is usable under the stated rule set.

PD-PPO passes the schedule-validity check in all ten seeds. Every logical channel is used at an intermediate duty cycle; no channel is permanently on or off. It outperforms the best original rule-based dynamic baseline in 10 out of 10 seeds and the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in 9 out of 10 seeds. The mean differences in FW-MAE (comparator minus PD-PPO) are $+0.008493$ and $+0.005477$, respectively, with paired one-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.00098$ and $p = 0.00488$ across seeds. The single exception under the duty-cycle rule is seed 45, where the rule-matched round-robin achieves FW-MAE of 0.145130 versus 0.148030 for PD-PPO; the PD-PPO schedule still satisfies all channel-usage checks.

The static baselines address a different question. The validation-selected static subset achieves zero switching by keeping three channels permanently on and five permanently off, and it splits the ten seeds evenly at 5/10 against PD-PPO. The static-priority baseline, which replays the same fixed channel priorities subject to the duty-cycle rule, is lower on average by 0.000320. This makes the static subset a strong fixed-design reference rather than a weak baseline. PD-PPO's main advantage is over rule-based dynamic schedules, while it maintains intermediate use of all eight channels.

Table 4 separates the main comparison by event status. The static-priority baseline is slightly lower on mean aggregate and event-conditioned FW-MAE, while PD-PPO uses more distinct feasible channel combinations and a less concentrated dominant selection. The high-cost laser disdrometer channel remains at an intermediate duty cycle (26.8% overall and 29.5% during events), showing that the learned schedule does not discard event-sensitive channels in order to reduce switching.

### 6.2. Budget and minimum-activation-time sensitivity

The same operating rules were evaluated at $B = 1.65$ and $B = 1.75$ (Table 5). PD-PPO passes the channel-usage check in all seeds at all three budget levels. It remains ahead of the best original rule-based dynamic baseline in 9/10, 10/10, and 9/10 seeds, and ahead of the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in 9/10, 9/10, and 7/10 seeds. The static-priority comparison is similar at $B = 1.65$ and $B = 1.70$, but the static-priority baseline gains a larger advantage at $B = 1.75$. The advantage over dynamic baselines is therefore stable across nearby budget levels, while fixed subset designs remain important references.

Table 6 varies the minimum activation time while keeping $B = 1.70$ and the same duty-cycle limits. All three settings produce valid PD-PPO schedules with eight intermediate-duty channels, no permanently on or off channels, and no warm-up aborts. A short minimum activation time of six steps allows fast rule-based dynamic baselines to approach PD-PPO. Minimum activation times of 12 and 24 steps remove that rapid-cycling advantage: PD-PPO outperforms the best original rule-based dynamic baseline in 10/10 seeds at both settings and outperforms the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in 10/10 seeds at 24 steps. The switching rate falls from 0.059 to 0.016 as the minimum activation time increases.

### 6.3. Training-guide ablation

Table 7 evaluates the two auxiliary training guides used by PD-PPO. Removing the candidate-policy KL guide raises mean FW-MAE from 0.140635 to 0.142476; the full PD-PPO is lower in 8 out of 10 paired seeds, with an average gain of 0.001841. Removing the advantage-weighted behavioural cloning term while keeping the KL guide has a larger effect: mean FW-MAE rises to 0.145448, the full PD-PPO is lower in 9 out of 10 paired seeds, and the win count against the best original rule-based dynamic baseline drops to 3/10. The PPO-only variant disables both guides while keeping the same forecast reward and action projection. It still passes the schedule-validity check, but its mean FW-MAE rises to 0.149170. These results show that forecast-derived training guidance substantially improves exploration over the combinatorial space of feasible channel subsets; the execution rules and held-out testing protocol are identical for all variants.

### 6.4. Fixed instantaneous budgets

Table 8 gives the fixed-budget benchmark over three budget levels and ten seeds per level. The full-observation reference achieves the lowest mean error at every budget, as expected. Among policies satisfying the reported budget, PD-PPO achieves lower mean FW-MAE than round-robin, AoI-based, and random feasible scheduling at all three budget levels.

The fixed-budget setting illustrates why static subset baselines are necessary. PD-PPO is significantly better than the three rule-based dynamic baselines after Bonferroni correction ($p \leq 0.0235$), while comparisons against the validation-selected static subset yield adjusted $p = 1.0$. A fixed subset can therefore be a strong design when the budget is purely instantaneous and the same low-cost channels carry most of the prediction signal.

### 6.5. Energy-account analysis

The energy-account analysis tests a different feasibility model in which the station can accumulate energy before a storm and spend it during event windows. Table 9 uses a reference-policy setting with normalised harvest $h = 0.92$, capacity $C = 180$, reserve 20, and budget $B = 1.20$.

The dynamic policy that switches from a snow-core channel set to a laser-plus-flux configuration during events reduces overall FW-MAE from 0.4248 to 0.4169 and event-period FW-MAE from 0.3517 to 0.3190 relative to the static snow-core baseline, without any energy-budget violations. The gain is concentrated in storm windows, where the expensive event-sensitive channels have episodic forecast value.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1. What the operating-rule results show

The experiment with operating rules supports the central claim of the paper: prediction-driven reinforcement learning can learn feasible channel selection strategies for sensor scheduling under a power budget when the action space enforces the operating rules. PD-PPO does not achieve its advantage over dynamic baselines by cycling channels rapidly or by abandoning expensive channels. Across ten seeds, every channel remains at an intermediate duty cycle and the switching rate stays low.

The strongest evidence comes from the comparison with dynamic baselines. Round-robin and AoI policies are natural alternatives because they either cycle through channels in a fixed order or prioritise channels with the largest AoI. PD-PPO outperforms the best original rule-based dynamic baseline in every seed and the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in nine seeds. The minimum-activation-time sweep clarifies why this comparison matters: when rapid switching is permitted, rule-based cycling becomes more competitive; when slower actuation is required, the learned policy maintains a clearer forecast-error advantage.

### 7.2. How to interpret the static baselines

The static subset baseline should not be treated as a weak reference. It is a validation-selected fixed design and therefore represents a strong option for systems where a small set of channels covers most of the forecast information. Its strength is useful: it prevents the learned scheduler from being evaluated only against cycling policies that may switch too frequently for the stated operating rules.

The static-priority baseline addresses a different question. It starts from the same validation-selected fixed channel combination but executes it subject to the duty-cycle rule. This baseline achieves low mean FW-MAE and remains close to PD-PPO, showing that fixed designs can remain competitive even after usage limits are imposed. The learned policy is distinguished by its advantage over dynamic baselines and by its balanced use of all channels, not by uniformly dominating every static reference.

### 7.3. Role of the auxiliary training guides

The ablation results show that the forecast reward alone is not sufficient. PPO with the same reward and projection can learn valid schedules, but it loses more often to the dynamic baselines. The candidate-policy KL guide and the advantage-weighted behavioural cloning term improve sample efficiency by steering exploration toward feasible channel subsets that have already demonstrated forecast value on the policy-training windows. The larger degradation after removing the behavioural cloning term indicates that labelled high-value actions are especially useful in this short-training, combinatorial-action setting.

This interpretation also explains why the method is framed as a scheduling system rather than as a generic PPO application. The contribution is the combination of forecast error as the reward signal, action projection for hard constraint satisfaction, chronological data separation, and baselines evaluated under matched operating rules.

### 7.4. Benchmark scope and limitations

The benchmark is a controlled simulator designed for scheduling analysis. It is calibrated against Antarctic station data and published blowing-snow statistics, and every policy is evaluated on the same ground-truth sequence for a given seed. This makes internal policy comparisons meaningful: differences in forecast error arise from the channel selection strategy, not from different meteorological realisations.

The simulator is nonetheless an abstraction. The normalised costs are scheduling costs, not measured watt-level loads. The event indicator is available in the controlled benchmark; a field deployment would require a station-side event detector or a probabilistic event forecast. Hardware transfer would also require measured startup times, power draw, sensor faults, and missing-data behaviour from the actual acquisition system. These considerations define the next validation stage without affecting the internal comparisons reported here.

### 7.5. Implications for sensing system design

The results suggest a practical design guideline. If an instantaneous power budget allows a small fixed channel subset to cover the forecast target, static allocation is a strong design and should be reported as a reference. If the system also has minimum activation time, duty-cycle, or switching constraints, learned scheduling can improve over AoI-based and cycling baselines while maintaining balanced channel use. The appropriate comparison is therefore not a learned policy against a single weak baseline, but a family of baselines that separately represents full observation, fixed subset design, dynamic rule-based policies, and dynamic policies subject to the same operating rules.

---

## 8. Conclusion

This paper studied sensor scheduling under a power budget for the forecasting objective. The proposed PD-PPO scheduler selects feasible channel subsets and is trained using the forecast error from a fixed, pre-trained forecasting model. The method enforces the power budget and operating rules at the point of action selection, and the evaluation protocol keeps forecasting model fitting, policy training, static reference selection, and final testing on strictly separate time periods.

In the Antarctic blowing-snow benchmark, PD-PPO learns valid, low-switching schedules that keep all eight logical channels in use at intermediate duty cycles. Across ten independent test runs, it outperforms the best rule-based dynamic baseline in every run and the best competing scheduler under the same duty-cycle rule in nine runs. Fixed subset baselines remain strong, particularly under instantaneous power budgets, and should be included in any evaluation of adaptive sensor scheduling.

The main conclusion is that prediction-driven reinforcement learning is effective when channel selection is judged by future forecast quality and when feasibility is enforced in the action layer. Under those conditions, a learned scheduler can consistently improve over standard rule-based dynamic baselines without producing unrealistic switching patterns or neglecting any channel.
