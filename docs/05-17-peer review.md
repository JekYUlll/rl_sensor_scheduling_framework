# Peer Review: "A Prediction-Driven Reinforcement Learning Framework for Adaptive Sensor Scheduling at Antarctic Automatic Weather Stations"

Manuscript submitted to: Cold Regions Science and Technology

Reviewer recommendation: Major Revision

### Summary

The manuscript proposes PD-PPO, a reinforcement learning framework for adaptive sensor scheduling at Antarctic automatic weather stations (AWSs) operating under tight power budgets. The core idea—optimising scheduling decisions directly against downstream forecast loss rather than proxy metrics such as Kalman-filter posterior covariance or Age-of-Information—is well-motivated and represents a genuine conceptual contribution. The paper is technically detailed, the experimental reporting is unusually transparent about development history (V2 vs. V3.1 generators), and the authors are commendably honest about the limitations of their ablation evidence. However, several fundamental concerns must be addressed before the manuscript is suitable for publication in Cold Regions Science and Technology.

### Major Concerns

1. Simulation-only validation with no real-field data

The most significant weakness for a journal focused on cold-regions science and technology is that the entire evaluation rests on a synthetic time series generator parameterised from published statistics (Wille et al., 2023; Amory, 2020), with no validation against real AWS measurements. The authors acknowledge this explicitly, noting that the hardware prototype "validates the sensing and communication path" but that "the scheduling controller, microcontroller, and relay array for closed-loop power switching are not yet implemented." The bench prototype logs are described as validating "sequence shapes expected by the simulator" rather than constituting an external validation dataset.

For a methods paper targeting a cold-regions audience, this is a critical gap. Readers of Cold Regions Science and Technology will reasonably ask whether the performance advantages demonstrated in simulation—3.2% improvement over round-robin, 12.1% over AoI—translate to any operationally meaningful benefit under real Antarctic conditions, including sensor drift, communication outages, icing events, and the highly non-stationary boundary-layer dynamics not captured by the DFT-phase-randomisation generator. The authors should either (a) provide at least a partial validation on real AWS data, even if limited in scope, or (b) substantially strengthen the discussion of how the synthetic environment's known limitations bound the generalisability of the reported results. The current limitations section (Section 7.3) is honest but insufficiently quantitative about this gap.

2. Marginal and partially insignificant performance gains

The primary result—PD-PPO achieves 3.2% lower FW-MAE than round-robin at B = 1.70—does not survive Bonferroni correction in the paired Wilcoxon test. The authors acknowledge this directly ("the round-robin margin is directionally consistent but smaller and does not survive Bonferroni correction uniformly"), but the abstract and conclusion present the 3.2% figure without this qualification. More importantly, PD-PPO is not significantly different from the feasible static projection baseline at any budget level. Given that the feasible static projection is a non-adaptive, fixed-priority rule, the inability to demonstrate statistically significant improvement over it raises a fundamental question about whether the adaptive RL machinery provides genuine value beyond a well-tuned static allocation. The authors discuss this in Section 7.2, but the discussion is somewhat defensive. A more rigorous treatment would quantify the conditions under which adaptive scheduling is expected to outperform static allocation (e.g., as a function of event frequency, warm-up latency, or budget tightness) and test whether those conditions are met in the V3.1 environment.

3. The "frozen oracle" reward design introduces a circularity that is not fully resolved

The prediction-driven reward is computed from a TCN oracle trained on the same synthetic data distribution used for policy training. The oracle is trained with a rollout mixture that oversamples full-observation conditions (42.9% of oracle pretraining steps). This means the oracle's forecast loss landscape is shaped by the full-observation regime, and the scheduler is rewarded for approximating that regime. The authors note that the oracle "achieves a forecast-weighted MAE of 0.1493 ± 0.0123 at budget B = 1.70 under full-observation conditions," establishing the performance ceiling. However, it is not clear that the oracle's loss surface is well-calibrated for the partial-observation regimes that the scheduler actually operates in. The oracle reward signal reliability diagnostic (Section 6.6) is conducted on the V2 generator rather than V3.1, and the absolute FW-MAE scales are explicitly stated to be incomparable. The authors should either (a) repeat the oracle monotonicity check on the V3.1 generator, or (b) provide a theoretical argument for why the V2 diagnostic is sufficient to validate the V3.1 reward signal.

4. The "Antarctic AWS" framing is partially misleading

The paper's title and framing prominently feature "Antarctic automatic weather stations," but the experimental system is a laboratory-scale simulation environment, not a deployed Antarctic station. The physical sensor suite described in Figure 1 appears to be a bench prototype assembled in Nanjing, not an instrument deployed in Antarctica or even in a polar climate. The blowing-snow events in the experiments are generated synthetically using statistics from Adélie Land (Amory, 2020) and Ny-Ålesund (Monrad-Krohn et al., 2026), not observed at the deployment site. The authors should either (a) retitle the paper to reflect the simulation-based nature of the study (e.g., "...for Simulation of Antarctic AWS Scheduling"), or (b) provide a clearer statement in the abstract and introduction distinguishing the target application domain from the actual experimental setting. The current framing risks misleading readers about the maturity of the system.

5. Appendix B is empty

Appendix B is titled "Hyperparameters" but contains no content. The hyperparameter table appears in the main text (Table B.9 in the appendix section, but rendered as part of the references section). This is a formatting error that must be corrected, and the appendix structure should be clarified.

Minor Concerns

1. The "School of [Department]" placeholder in the author affiliation must be completed. This is an obvious oversight that should be corrected before resubmission.
2. The proof sketches for Propositions 1 and 2 are informal. Proposition 1 is stated as a formal impossibility result but the proof sketch relies on a specific two-variable construction. The authors should either (a) provide a complete proof in the appendix, or (b) restate Proposition 1 as a constructive example rather than a universal impossibility claim. The current phrasing ("There is no monotone transformation that, for all warm-up-aware sensor schedules...") is stronger than what the proof sketch establishes.
3. The EventAwareCritic degrades performance in the staged ablation (D1→D2: 0.1821→0.1939). The authors acknowledge this and describe the component as a "mechanistic stabiliser," but the explanation is not fully satisfying. If the EventAwareCritic consistently increases FW-MAE in the staged path, its inclusion in the final architecture requires stronger justification. The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of when and why the EventAwareCritic helps or hurts, or consider removing it from the proposed architecture if it cannot be shown to provide consistent benefit.
4. The DQN baseline is evaluated on the V2 generator, not V3.1. The authors explicitly state that "its strongest run was produced during the V2 development branch" and exclude it from the V3.1 main-result table. This means the comparison between PD-PPO and DQN is not conducted on the same experimental platform. The DQN analysis in Section 7.1 is presented as a diagnostic, but it is used to motivate architectural choices in PD-PPO. A V3.1-aligned DQN result, even if preliminary, would strengthen this motivation.
5.  The target weights β = (1.0, 1.0, 1.2, 0.6, 0.6, 1.0, 3.0, 2.0, 2.0) are not justified. The snow mass flux and particle variables receive weights of 3.0 and 2.0 respectively, which substantially influence the FW-MAE metric. The rationale for these specific values is not provided. Were they determined by cross-validation, domain expertise, or sensitivity analysis? The hyperparameter sensitivity section (Section 6.11) evaluates only λ_AWBC and λ_KL; a sensitivity analysis on the target weights would strengthen confidence in the reported results.
6.  The normalised power cost units are not transparently derived. The authors state that "power values are normalised deployment-cost units informed by datasheet electrical characteristics, warm-up latency, burst/peak behaviour, and forecast-channel scarcity, not by linearly rescaling average wattage." However, the specific mapping from these factors to the values in Table 1 is not described. This makes it difficult for readers to adapt the framework to different sensor configurations. A more explicit description of the cost assignment procedure, even if qualitative, would improve reproducibility.
7.  The reference "Al Ahdab et al., 2025" cites arXiv:2507.11240, which has a preprint identifier suggesting it was posted in July 2025. The authors should verify that this reference is correctly dated and that the arXiv identifier is accurate, as the numbering convention (2507.xxxxx) would place it in July 2025, which is consistent with the 2025 year cited.

### Recommendation

The paper addresses a genuinely interesting problem and the methodological contributions—particularly the CMDP formalisation with prediction-driven reward and the AWBC/oracle-prior stabilisation mechanism—are technically sound. However, the combination of simulation-only validation, marginal and partially insignificant performance gains over baselines, and the misleading "Antarctic AWS" framing in the absence of any Antarctic deployment data represents a significant gap between the paper's claims and its evidence. The authors are encouraged to resubmit after addressing the major concerns, particularly by either providing real-data validation or substantially revising the framing and claims to accurately reflect the simulation-based nature of the study.
